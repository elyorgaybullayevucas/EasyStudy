L: Good afternoon. Today I want to look at one of the more surprising success stories in urban ecology, which is beekeeping in cities. Let me start with some background. Honeybees matter to all of us because they pollinate about a third of the crops we eat, from apples to almonds. Yet in the countryside, where you would expect them to thrive, colonies have been declining for decades. The two main causes are well known: the heavy use of pesticides on farmland, and the loss of habitat, as hedges and wild meadows have been replaced by large single crop fields.
L: Now, why would a city suit bees better than a field? The first reason is variety. A modern farm may offer one kind of flower for two weeks and then nothing. A city, on the other hand, has a huge range of flowering plants in its parks and gardens, blooming from early spring to late autumn. The second reason is temperature. Because of all the buildings and roads, a city is typically about two degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside, so the foraging season is longer. And thirdly, far fewer agricultural chemicals are used in cities, so the bees are simply healthier.
L: Let me give you a case study. Paris is probably the most famous beekeeping city in Europe. Hives have been kept on the roof of the opera house since 1985, and there are now hundreds more on offices, schools and even the roofs of department stores. Beekeepers there report that honey production per hive can be twice as high as in rural areas, and the honey itself has a more complex flavour because of the variety of flowers.
L: It isn't all good news, of course. When beekeeping becomes fashionable, you get too many hives in one area, and they compete for the same flowers. Crowded hives also make it much easier for disease to spread from one colony to the next. And there's a more subtle issue. Honeybees are only one species. Cities are also home to many kinds of wild bees, and a large number of honeybee hives can push these out. So the advice from ecologists is that wherever hives are introduced, wildflowers should be planted as well, so that there is enough food for all species.
L: Finally, a word for anyone who's thinking of starting. Don't rush out and buy a hive. Join a local association first, where experienced keepers will teach you how to handle the bees safely and how to recognise problems early. Most cities now have one, and many offer courses for beginners. Right, let's move on to how a colony is organised.
